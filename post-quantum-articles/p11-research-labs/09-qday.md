# Q-Day Is a Forcing Function, Not a Funeral

Education · May 26, 2026

![Forcing Function](images/09-qday.jpg)

**The deadline that will force the most significant upgrade to digital security infrastructure in a generation.**

The dominant story about quantum computing and cryptography is a disaster story. A cryptographically relevant quantum computer switches on, Bitcoin collapses, custody fails, and the digital asset ecosystem unwinds in an afternoon.

That framing is not only wrong. It is counterproductive.

The real story is more interesting and more hopeful. Q-Day, when it comes, will not be a funeral. It will be the deadline that forced the largest upgrade to digital security infrastructure in a generation — and the industry that prepares for it will come out stronger.

## Forcing functions produce progress

Cryptographic upgrades are rarely smooth or proactive. SSL 3.0 was retired because an attack made it untenable. SHA-1 was retired because researchers demonstrated practical collisions. Old TLS versions disappeared because browser vendors drew a hard line. In each case an external push achieved what voluntary upgrade cycles had not.

## HTTPS, the instructive example

For years, unencrypted web traffic was the default. The threat was understood and the fix existed, yet adoption stalled. Then search ranking began to reward HTTPS and browsers began labelling plain HTTP as not secure. Within a few years, encrypted traffic went from minority to norm — and the push produced free, automated certificate authorities, certificate transparency, and a generation of developers who treat encryption as a baseline.

> The absence of a visible crisis will be the measure of whether we did the work.

## What the upgrade cycle produces

- **Crypto agility.** Systems learn to treat cryptography as a replaceable component rather than a fixed assumption, which makes them resilient to any future break.
- **Better key management.** Address reuse, key exposure and derivation-path hygiene stop being ignorable.
- **Stronger standards.** An eight-year public competition produced the most heavily vetted signature schemes ever standardised, with more on the way.
- **New primitives.** Lattice, hash-based and isogeny constructions that were academic curiosities a decade ago are now production-grade — and may unlock capabilities we have not imagined yet.

## The blockchain case

Blockchains are uniquely hard to migrate: everything is public, permanent and multilateral. That difficulty is also the opportunity. Protocols that prove they can execute a network-wide security upgrade without breaking anything will have demonstrated exactly the capability institutions are looking for — and they will set the standards everyone downstream adopts.

Q-Day is probably closer than most people think. That is not a reason for despair. It is a reason to move.
